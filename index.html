Part one — the work

What pyGAM is, and what I set out to fix

A Generalized Additive Model (GAM) fits flexible curves instead of straight lines — it lets the data decide the shape of each feature's effect, rather than assuming everything is linear. pyGAM is the most widely used library for building these models in Python, maintained by Daniel Servén and used across research and industry.

After fitting a model, pyGAM reports a p-value for each curve. That number is meant to answer one honest question:

Is this wiggle a real pattern in the data, or could plain noise have produced something that looks just as structured?

A small p-value means "noise rarely does this — the effect is probably real." A large one means "can't tell it apart from chance." These numbers are what turn a pretty curve into a defensible scientific claim.

the problem

The bug: p-values you couldn't trust

Issue #163 reported that pyGAM's p-values were anti-conservative: instead of spreading out honestly, they piled up near zero. In practice that means the model calls almost everything "significant" — including pure noise. A p-value that cries wolf is worse than no p-value at all, because it hands you confident, wrong conclusions.

The correct method comes from a 2013 paper by Simon Wood — the author of R's mgcv, the reference implementation for GAMs that the whole field trusts. My project was to bring that method faithfully into pyGAM, and then prove the new p-values behave.

the solution

Five things were wrong. Here's each one.

Every fix is a specific, self-contained correction to how the p-value is computed — kept short here; the details live in part two.

1

Effective degrees of freedom

A penalised curve is less flexible than its number of coefficients suggests — the penalty holds it back. That true flexibility has to be measured as tr(2F − F²), not tr(F), and — the part earlier attempts missed — actually used in the test rather than computed and discarded.

2

Pseudoinverse truncation

The test inverts the curve's covariance, but some directions are so heavily penalised they're effectively null. Inverting their near-zero eigenvalues divides by almost nothing and inflates the statistic into false significance. The fix drops those directions below a numerical threshold before inverting.

3

The reference distribution

Because the effective df is fractional, the statistic doesn't follow a tidy χ². It follows a weighted mixture of chi-squareds with no closed form, so I use the Liu–Tang–Zhang moment-matching approximation Wood recommends — not the wrong χ²(rank).

4

QR-based computation

The statistic lives on the fitted curve, not the raw coefficients. Computing it through a QR decomposition of the term's model matrix keeps it in "curve space" — faster, and far more stable than forming and inverting the full covariance. This was the subtlest part, and where an early version hid a bug that only looked correct.

5

Estimated-scale families

For Gaussian-type models the noise level is estimated from the data, which adds uncertainty the p-value must respect. Instead of an F-shortcut, I integrate over that uncertainty by quadrature, properly accounting for the extra randomness.

my contribution

What I actually wrote

The work touches pyGAM's inference core and adds a validation layer around it. Concretely:

In pygam/pygam.py

  • Rewrote _compute_p_value to use Wood's method end-to-end.
  • Added _woodteststat — the curve-space test statistic (the heart of it).
  • Added _liu2 — the Liu–Tang–Zhang chi-squared mixture approximation.
  • Added _liu2_scaled_quadrature — the estimated-scale correction.
  • Fixed the effective-df computation to the correct tr(2F − F²) form.

In pygam/tests/test_pvalues.py — authored from scratch

  • 88 tests across the whole pipeline: unit checks, numeric agreement with compiled mgcv (13 cases), null-calibration (type-I error), power, tensor terms, and a real-dataset check.
  • Plus standalone validation scripts: null calibration, known-signal, and a reproduction of Wood's Figure 2 grid.

Everything is in pull request #586 against the main pyGAM repository.

angle one — honesty

Null calibration: the QQ-plot

The definitive test of an honest p-value: fit a smooth on pure noise hundreds of times and collect the p-value each time. If the method is calibrated, those p-values must be uniform on [0,1] — which means, on a QQ-plot, they land on the diagonal. This is a direct reproduction of Figure 2 in Wood's paper, and it's the single clearest picture that the bug is gone.

I ran the same check across Wood's full grid — univariate and tensor smooths, two sample sizes, Gaussian and binary responses — and it reproduces the paper's finding: everything is calibrated at larger samples, with only the small-sample binary case wobbling, exactly as Wood reports.

angle two — power & agreement

It detects what's real, and matches the reference

Calibration only proves the method stays quiet on noise. It also has to be loud on real effects — and it is: on data with a genuine signal, the p-value fires essentially every time, and it falls smoothly as the signal grows stronger (a clean dose-response). Separately, on 13 matched cases the statistic agrees with compiled mgcv to about nine decimal places — including a real tensor (two-dimensional) smooth and a categorical factor term.

angle three — real data

And it recovers the textbook answers

The final check uses genuine data with a known answer: the classic ISLR wage dataset. The recovered p-values match decades of established analysis — age strongly nonlinear, education strong, year weak but real — and the categorical factor term works correctly, closing the last unverified path in the code.

where it stands

Done, and what's next

The five fixes are implemented, the p-values are calibrated and powerful, and the statistic matches the reference implementation to nine decimals across every term type pyGAM supports. What's left is documentation and a changelog — and one stretch goal (a smoothing-parameter uncertainty correction) that the proposal always framed as optional. The hard part is finished; the numbers are honest.
