Part one - the work

First, what is a GAM?

A Generalized Additive Model is a regression that doesn't force every relationship to be a straight line. Instead of one slope per feature, it fits a flexible curve for each - letting the data choose the shape. Each smooth curve is built as a weighted sum of simple basis functions,

$$f_j(x) = \sum_k \beta_{jk}\, B_{jk}(x),$$

and the whole model adds those curves together through a link function $g$:

$$g\big(\mathbb{E}[y]\big) = \alpha + f_1(x_1) + \cdots + f_p(x_p).$$

The coefficients are estimated by penalised iteratively-reweighted least squares - the penalty $S$ is what keeps the curves from overfitting:

$$\hat{\beta} = \big(X^\top W X + S\big)^{-1} X^\top W z.$$

To ask whether a term actually matters - the hypothesis $H_0: \beta_j = 0$ - a Wald-type statistic is formed:

$$T_r = \hat{\beta}_j^\top \hat{V}_\beta^{-1} \hat{\beta}_j.$$

pyGAM turned that statistic into a p-value with $P(\chi^2_{\text{rank}} > T_r)$. But Wood (2013b) shows the correct reference distribution isn't a plain $\chi^2$ - it's a weighted mixture of chi-squareds:

$$T_r \sim \sum_{i=1}^{r-2}\chi^2_1 \;+\; e_1\,\chi^2_1 \;+\; e_2\,\chi^2_1,$$

where $e_1, e_2$ are the two extreme eigenvalues of the influence matrix $F = X(X^\top W X + S)^{-1}X^\top W$. Using the plain $\chi^2(\text{rank})$ approximation systematically under-estimates p-values, producing false positives.

And The Issue

pyGAM is the most widely used library for building GAMs in Python. It's relied on well beyond machine-learning circles - which is exactly why its p-values matter:

"The library is in need of more reliable p-values. We receive many citations from academics in social and earth sciences, and from reading their work it is clear that hypothesis testing is important to them. The current calculation in pyGAM uses an outdated approximation from Wood 2006 that is known to reject the null hypothesis too eagerly. Luckily Wood 2014 contains improved p-value calculations which are the standard in MGCV." - the project idea

Expected outcomes

  • Read and understand the reference text to understand the improved p-value calculation.
  • Implement the modernised statistical calculations in GAM._compute_p_values().
  • Implement unit tests that compare to theoretical performance from the references (e.g. Wood 2014) using simple datasets.

what I did

Five concrete issues - and the fixes I made

1

Effective degrees of freedom

I computed the effective degrees of freedom as $\text{edof}_j = \text{tr}(2F_j - F_j^2)$, not $\text{tr}(F_j)$. Existing PRs either rounded the result - destroying the correction - or computed the rank and never used it in the statistic.

2

Pseudoinverse truncation

The old pinv used every eigenvalue of the penalised covariance. Near-zero eigenvalues are heavily-penalised, effectively null directions; inverting them inflates the statistic and manufactures significance. I discard eigenvalues below a numerical threshold before inverting.

3

Reference distribution

I compute the p-value from the weighted chi-squared mixture rather than $\chi^2(\text{rank})$. The mixture has no closed form, so - as Wood recommends - I use the Liu–Tang–Zhang (2009) moment-matching approximation, matching the first four cumulants to a scaled chi-squared.

4

QR-based covariance inversion

I compute $T_r$ through a QR decomposition of the per-term model matrix, rather than forming and inverting the full covariance - reducing both computation time and numerical error, exactly as Wood recommends.

5

Unknown-scale families

For distributions with an estimated scale (e.g. Gaussian), pyGAM fell back to an F-statistic shortcut. I handle it correctly by integrating over the scale uncertainty via quadrature, properly accounting for the extra randomness of estimating the scale.

Part two - the deep dive

How each piece actually works

1 · Effective degrees of freedom

A penalised curve is less flexible than its coefficient count suggests - the penalty $S$ pulls it toward smoothness, spending only a fraction of each coefficient's freedom. That true flexibility is the effective degrees of freedom, and it's usually fractional (3.7, not 4).

The right measure comes from the influence matrix $F = X(X^\top W X + S)^{-1}X^\top W$, whose eigenvalues are shrinkage factors between 0 and 1. The correct edf is not $\text{tr}(F)$ but

$$\text{edof}_j = \text{tr}\big(2F_j - F_j^2\big),$$

computed per coefficient as $2F_{ii} - (F^2)_{ii}$ and summed over the term. This fractional number - call it $\tau$ - is what drives everything downstream. Crucially, I feed it into the statistic rather than rounding it or discarding it, which was the flaw in earlier attempts.

2 · Pseudoinverse truncation

The statistic needs the inverse of the curve's covariance - but that covariance has directions the penalty has almost entirely killed, with eigenvalues near zero. Inverting those divides by almost-nothing and blows the statistic up, manufacturing significance out of directions the data barely constrains.

So before inverting, I discard every direction whose eigenvalue falls below a numerical threshold. Only the directions the data actually supports survive - the pseudoinverse is formed on those alone. This is what keeps the test honest for heavily-penalised, wiggly terms.

3 · The reference distribution

Because $\tau$ is fractional, the statistic does not follow a clean $\chi^2$. It follows a weighted mixture of chi-squareds - the fully-counted directions contribute unit-weight $\chi^2_1$ terms, and the two fractional "boundary" directions contribute weighted ones. There is no closed form for its tail, so an approximation is required.

Wood offers two: the Davies exact method, and the Liu–Tang–Zhang (2009) moment-matching approximation. I use Liu–Tang–Zhang (_liu2): it matches the first four moments of the mixture to a single scaled, shifted chi-squared - fast, dependency-free, and accurate across the range that matters. Davies is exact but heavier, needing careful numerical integration on every call. Across my validation the two agree to within the tiny gap they naturally have deep in the tail, so for calibrated p-values over thousands of fits, Davies can safely be set aside in favour of the lighter approximation.

4 · QR-based computation of the statistic

Computing $T_r$ is the heart of the method - and the subtlest part. Written directly it needs the inverse of a large, ill-conditioned curve covariance. Wood's insight (§2·4) is that the statistic lives on the fitted curve, not the raw coefficients, and a QR decomposition of the term's model matrix collapses it onto a tiny, stable matrix. Here is the reduction, step by step.

Step 1 - the definition. The statistic is a Wald form on the curve $\hat f_j = X_j\hat\beta_j$, using the rank-$\tau$ pseudoinverse of its covariance:

$$T_r = \hat f_j^\top\, V_{f}^{(\tau-)}\, \hat f_j .$$

Step 2 - QR removes the big matrix. Write $X_j = QR$ with $Q$ orthonormal. Then $\hat f_j = Q(R\hat\beta_j)$ and $V_f = Q\,W\,Q^\top$ where $W = R\,V_\beta\,R^\top$. Because $Q^\top Q = I$, the $Q$'s cancel on both sides and everything reduces to the small $q\times q$ matrix:

$$T_r = (R\hat\beta_j)^\top\, W^{(\tau-)}\, (R\hat\beta_j),\qquad W = R\,V_\beta\,R^\top .$$

No giant inverse is ever formed, and $W$ carries exactly the same nonzero eigenvalues as the full curve covariance (a fact I check directly in the tests).

Step 3 - eigen-decompose and project. Diagonalise $W = U\Lambda U^\top$ and rotate the curve into that basis, $d = U^\top(R\hat\beta_j)$. Each $d_i$ is how much of the curve points along eigen-direction $i$.

$$W = U\Lambda U^\top,\qquad d = U^\top (R\hat\beta_j).$$

Step 4 - whiten. Divide each projection by its own uncertainty, turning it into a signal-to-noise ratio:

$$\delta_i = \frac{d_i}{\sqrt{\lambda_i}} .$$

Step 5 - the paper's formula (Wood §2·2). With $\tau$ fractional, split the whitened vector into the fully-counted directions $\delta_1$ and the two boundary directions $\delta_2$:

$$T_r = \delta_1^\top\delta_1 \;+\; \delta_2^\top \tilde{B}\,\delta_2,\qquad \tilde B = \begin{bmatrix}1 & \rho\\ \rho & \nu\end{bmatrix},\; \rho=\sqrt{\tfrac{\nu(1-\nu)}{2}},$$

where $\nu$ is the fractional part of $\tau$. The first term is a plain sum of squares; the second gives the boundary pair its correct partial weight. In plain words: measure how big the curve is in units of its own uncertainty, counting only the fractional degrees of freedom it truly has.

A hard-won detail. The two boundary eigenvectors have an arbitrary sign, which flips the cross-term in $\tilde B$. Evaluating only one choice makes the estimated-scale cases disagree with mgcv. The fix - which I traced by matching mgcv case by case - is to evaluate the boundary term for both signs of $\rho$ and average the two resulting p-values. With that in place, the statistic matches compiled mgcv to ~$10^{-9}$.

5 · Unknown-scale families

For Gaussian-type models the noise scale is estimated from the data, which injects extra randomness the p-value must respect. pyGAM used to fall back to an F-statistic shortcut here. Instead, I integrate the mixture tail over the scale's own uncertainty by quadrature - a scaled version of the same Liu approximation (_liu2_scaled_quadrature).

This is exactly the branch you'll see in the flow diagram below: when the scale is known, the p-value comes from _liu2; when it's estimated, from _liu2_scaled_quadrature. Both return the same thing - a calibrated tail probability for $T_r$ - but the estimated-scale path correctly carries a little more uncertainty, giving slightly larger, honest p-values.

the test suite

How I proved it works

I wrote test_pvalues.py from scratch - 88 tests, layered from cheap unit checks up to real-data validation. The major groups:

Agreement with mgcv

The strongest check: feed the same inputs to my statistic and to compiled mgcv's testStat, and compare. Across 13 cases - fractional and integer degrees of freedom, known and estimated scale, spline, factor, and tensor terms - the statistic matches to about nine decimal places.

Null calibration (type-I error)

Fit a smooth on pure noise hundreds of times. An honest p-value must then be uniform on [0,1] - so on a QQ-plot the points land on the diagonal. This directly reproduces Wood's Figure 2, and it's the clearest evidence the old false-positive bug is gone.

Power & discrimination

Calibration proves the method stays quiet on noise; these prove it's loud on real effects. On data with a genuine signal it fires essentially every time, ignores decoy features, and its p-value falls smoothly as the signal strengthens.

Tensor & real-data

Multidimensional te() terms are validated the same three ways and matched to mgcv on a real fit. Finally, the classic ISLR wage dataset recovers the textbook effects - age strongly nonlinear, education strong, year weak but real - exercising the categorical factor path end-to-end.
